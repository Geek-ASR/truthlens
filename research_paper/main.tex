\begin{abstract}
Short-form video is now a primary vector for political misinformation, yet
most automated fact-checking research targets text -- tweets, news
sentences, or curated claim lists -- and most LLM-based fact-checking
systems are evaluated on whether they reach a correct final label, not on
whether the intermediate evidence chain that produced that label is real.
We present TruthLens, a fact-checking pipeline for short-form political
video (Instagram Reels, in the deployed case study) that enforces a strict
evidence-before-verdict ordering -- atomic claim decomposition, multi-query
web research, tiered source retrieval, per-source evidence extraction, and
only then a claim-level verdict -- and, distinctively, subjects every
LLM-generated verdict to a deterministic, non-LLM validation pass before
it can be published. The validator checks that every cited piece of
evidence actually exists, that every cited source was actually fetched,
and that every number asserted in the model's reasoning actually appears
in a cited passage; verdicts that fail are automatically downgraded rather
than silently trusted. We further introduce an explicit distinction
between \texttt{UNVERIFIED} (evidence was genuinely sought and found
insufficient) and \texttt{RESEARCH\_FAILED} (the retrieval infrastructure
itself broke), so that an infrastructure failure can never be published as
if it were a considered null result, and a cost-aware model-escalation
pattern in which a free, locally-hosted model is used by default and a
paid model is invoked only when a deterministic ``is this output
substantively grounded'' check fails. We report preliminary operational
telemetry collected during iterative development -- including that
deterministic validation downgraded 28.6\% (6 of 21) of generated verdicts
in our test corpus, and that the free local model required escalation on
25--43\% of invocations depending on pipeline stage -- and are explicit
about what these numbers do and do not establish: they come from a small,
non-independent, single-domain test corpus accumulated during debugging,
not a held-out benchmark. A small first step toward a real held-out
check -- two reels TruthLens had never seen, each independently
fact-checked by a professional Indian fact-checking organization --
found its verdict agreeing with the independent ground truth in both
cases, but also surfaced a more consequential result: on both, the
system's text-derived claim extraction missed the actual visual
misinformation (real footage recaptioned with a false claim about what
it shows), and one run exposed a real gap in validation coverage that
let a downgraded verdict's hallucinated reasoning reach a rendered
slide, which we traced and fixed the same day. We describe the larger
evaluation this motivates and a taxonomy of concrete failure modes
discovered through live testing that we believe generalize to other
LLM-pipeline fact-checking systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Political misinformation increasingly spreads through short-form video --
Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, TikTok -- rather than text
\cite{shang2021tiktec}. This shift matters for automated fact-checking
research in two ways that are easy to underestimate. First, the input is
multi-modal and lossy: a claim may be spoken (transcript), displayed as
on-screen text (OCR), or only implied by a caption, and any system that
treats a reel as a single block of text discards exactly the structure a
fact-checker needs to attribute who said what. Second, and more
consequentially, the dominant existing evaluation paradigm for automated
fact-checking -- did the system output the correct veracity label for a
claim, measured against a benchmark such as FEVER \cite{thorne2018fever}
or LIAR \cite{wang2017liar} -- says nothing about whether the evidence the
system cites in support of that label is real. An LLM asked to
``fact-check this claim'' can produce a fluent, well-cited-looking verdict
whose citations point to sources it never retrieved, or whose numbers
never appeared in any source it read. This is the specific failure mode
this paper is about.

We built and iteratively debugged TruthLens, a pipeline that ingests a
political short-form video, decomposes it into atomic factual claims,
researches each claim against the open web, and produces a
carousel-style, cited, claim-by-claim fact-check -- while trying to make
that specific failure mode (correct-sounding but ungrounded output)
structurally difficult rather than merely discouraged by prompting. The
central design principle, stated as a hard constraint throughout
development, is \textbf{evidence before verdict}: the system is not
permitted to propose a verdict and then search for support; it must
search first, and a verdict can only be built from evidence rows that
already exist in the database at the time the verdict-proposal model is
invoked.

Enforcing this ordering architecturally is necessary but not sufficient,
because even a model given only real, already-fetched evidence can still
hallucinate -- inventing a citation to evidence that was never actually
supplied, asserting a number the source never stated, or (a failure mode
we discovered live, not anticipated in advance) padding a
\texttt{reasoning\_summary} field entirely with internal citation markup
and no actual prose, which is schema-valid (a non-empty string) while
being substantively empty. TruthLens's second contribution is therefore a
deterministic, non-LLM validation layer that re-checks every LLM output
against ground truth already in the database before it is allowed to
reach a reader, downgrading rather than trusting outputs that fail.

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item An architecture and reference implementation for evidence-grounded,
  multi-claim fact-checking of short-form video, including atomic claim
  decomposition with verbatim-quote grounding, multi-tier source
  credibility scoring, and deterministic (non-LLM) aggregation of
  per-claim verdicts into a single auditable reel-level verdict
  (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).
\item A deterministic post-hoc validation methodology -- citation
  existence, source-fetch existence, and number-grounding checks -- that
  catches a measurable fraction of ungrounded LLM output without
  requiring a second, equally fallible LLM call to police the first
  (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
\item An explicit \texttt{RESEARCH\_FAILED} vs.\ \texttt{UNVERIFIED}
  distinction, and a publication gate that refuses to let infrastructure
  failure masquerade as a considered null result -- an instance of the
  broader selective-prediction principle that a system should distinguish
  ``I looked and found nothing'' from ``I could not look''
  (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
\item A cost-aware escalation pattern -- default to a free, local model;
  escalate to a paid model only when a deterministic substantiveness
  check fails -- evaluated, as preliminary telemetry, on how often
  escalation actually triggers per pipeline stage
  (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).
\item A concrete taxonomy of failure modes discovered through live,
  iterative testing against a real deployed reel, several of which (a
  paraphrase misattributed as a direct quote; a dashboard verdict that
  silently disagreed with the published carousel verdict; a
  bracket-matching validation-note regex that truncated at the wrong
  bracket) are not, to our knowledge, previously documented in the
  automated fact-checking literature at this level of implementation
  detail (Section~\ref{sec:failuremodes}).
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit throughout about the evidentiary status of our claims.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports real, measured telemetry from
TruthLens's own audit log, not simulated or projected numbers -- but that
telemetry comes from a small corpus (three distinct source videos, 16
distinct claims, 21 verdict-generation events including re-runs across
pipeline-fix iterations) accumulated during debugging rather than a
frozen, held-out benchmark, and we describe in
Section~\ref{sec:futurework} the larger evaluation required before any
accuracy claim here should be treated as representative.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

Automated fact-checking and LLM factuality evaluation have both grown
into large enough literatures to warrant their own surveys
\cite{rahman2025hallucinationtotruth,vykopal2024genaisurvey}; we focus below
on the specific sub-areas TruthLens draws on and departs from, rather
than attempting a comprehensive survey ourselves.

\subsection{Claim detection and decomposition}
Claim detection for fact-checking spans monolingual, multilingual, and
cross-lingual settings \cite{panchendrarajan2024claimdetection}, all of which are
relevant to TruthLens's mixed English/Hindi/Urdu input. ClaimBuster
\cite{hassan2017claimbuster} established check-worthy claim
detection as a distinct pipeline stage, using a TF-IDF/POS/NER-featured
SVM to rank sentences from political debate transcripts by how worth
fact-checking they are. TruthLens's claim-extraction stage performs a
related but distinct task: rather than ranking existing sentences, an LLM
decomposes a transcript, OCR stream, and caption into new atomic
claims, splitting compound statements (e.g. ``X happened, and because of
it Y happened'') into independently checkable parts, following the
general claim-decomposition strategy used in systems such as ProgramFC,
which decomposes complex claims into sub-questions before per-question
retrieval. We extend this with a grounding requirement specific to
video: each extracted claim's \texttt{source\_quote}, when present, must
be a verbatim substring of the actual transcript/OCR text, checked
deterministically, not merely asserted by the model.

\subsection{Fact-checking benchmarks}
FEVER \cite{thorne2018fever} (Wikipedia-derived claims, Supports/
Refutes/NotEnoughInfo) and LIAR \cite{wang2017liar} (12.8K PolitiFact
statements, six-way truthfulness) are the dominant benchmarks for
claim-verification models, and both evaluate a system's final label
against ground truth without examining whether the evidence chain that
produced the label is real. TruthLens's evaluation gap
(Section~\ref{sec:futurework}) is precisely that we do not yet have a
FEVER/LIAR-scale held-out benchmark for our own domain (Indian political
short-form video); closing that gap, likely by curating a small
labeled set in the style of these benchmarks rather than reusing them
directly (our claims arrive multi-modally and in Hindi/Urdu/English
mixed transcripts, which neither benchmark covers), is the single most
important item of future work.

\subsection{LLM hallucination and grounding verification}
A growing body of work targets citation and grounding hallucination in
LLM output specifically. A preregistered, hand-scored evaluation of
leading commercial legal AI research tools found each hallucinates on
17--33\% of queries \cite{magesh2024hallucinationfree}, and recent
systems such as CiteCheck \cite{khajavi2026citecheck} detect hallucinated
references by grounding each citation against external sources with a
structured LLM verifier. Our validation layer (Section~\ref{sec:methodology})
shares the goal but differs in mechanism: rather than using a second LLM
call to judge whether a citation is plausible, TruthLens's checks are
deterministic set-membership and substring tests against data the
pipeline itself already fetched (does the cited \texttt{evidence\_id}
exist; was the cited source actually retrieved; does the asserted number
appear in the cited passage's stored text). This trades generality (we
cannot catch a hallucination that happens to reuse a real, cited evidence
ID incorrectly) for auditability and zero marginal LLM cost per check. A
recent analysis of citation-grounding oracles in the legal domain is a
useful caution for exactly this kind of check: it shows that an apparent
hallucination rate measured by verifying citations against a reference
graph is highly sensitive to that graph's coverage, not just to the
model being measured, with the same responses scoring 15--21\%
``hallucinated'' against a sparse snapshot of a citation registry and
under 1.1\% against a denser one built from the same source
\cite{ovcharov2026oracle}. TruthLens's own number-grounding check has an
analogous blind spot: it can only confirm a number against sources the
pipeline itself fetched, so it cannot distinguish ``this number is
fabricated'' from ``this number is real but supported only by a source
we did not retrieve.'' We flag this as an open limitation rather than a
solved problem.

\subsection{Political fact-checking with LLMs}
Most directly relevant to TruthLens's domain, DeVerna et al.\
\cite{deverna2025curatedcontext} evaluated 15 LLMs against over 6,000
PolitiFact-verified claims and found that reasoning ability provided
``minimal benefit'' and raw web search only ``moderate gains'' for
political fact-checking accuracy, while providing curated, high-quality
context improved macro-F1 by an average of 233\% across models. This is
strong independent evidence for TruthLens's core design bet: that
pipeline-level evidence curation (multi-query research planning, source
tiering, per-source relevance filtering) matters more than which LLM
does the final reasoning step. We view TruthLens as, in part, an
applied instantiation and extension of that finding to a harder,
multi-modal input domain.

\subsection{Short-form video misinformation}
TikTec \cite{shang2021tiktec} detects misleading COVID-19 short videos on
TikTok by jointly modeling visual, audio, and textual signal, using
captions to focus attention on the most informative frames. Recent work
on checkworthiness detection specifically targets multilingual
short-form video \cite{vatndal2025shortcheck}. TruthLens differs
from this line of work in task framing: rather than a binary/graded
misinformation classifier over the video as a whole, TruthLens performs
open-domain, claim-level, cited verification against live web evidence,
closer in spirit to FEVER-style verification than to TikTec-style
classification, but applied to video-derived claims rather than
Wikipedia sentences.

\subsection{Political neutrality of automated fact-checkers}
LLMs are not neutral instruments; recent evaluations document
measurable partisan asymmetries in frontier models under politically
charged prompts \cite{peng2026partisan}. Because a visibly biased
automated fact-checker is worse than none for a politically sensitive
application, every TruthLens system prompt includes an explicit
neutrality clause instructing the model to apply identical evidentiary
standards regardless of which political actor is named and to be
equally willing to conclude a claim is true or false for any side. We
have not run a systematic bias audit of TruthLens's own output (see
Section~\ref{sec:futurework}); this is a limitation, not a resolved
claim.

\subsection{Cost-aware model cascading}
FrugalGPT \cite{chen2023frugalgpt} demonstrated that routing most queries
to a cheap model and escalating only hard cases to an expensive one can
sharply cut inference cost with little accuracy loss. TruthLens applies
the same principle in reverse-priority form suited to a zero-marginal-cost
constraint: every stage defaults to a \emph{free, locally hosted} model
(Llama 3.2 via Ollama) rather than a cheap paid one, and escalates to a
paid model (Gemini) only when a deterministic quality gate -- not a
learned cost/accuracy predictor as in FrugalGPT -- detects that the local
model's output is schema-valid but substantively empty.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

TruthLens is a FastAPI backend with a PostgreSQL/pgvector datastore, a
Next.js review dashboard, and an object store (S3-compatible) for media
and rendered output. The pipeline runs in the following strict order for
every reel, and no stage may begin before the stage before it has
persisted its output to the database:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ingestion.} The source video is downloaded (or manually
  uploaded), audio is extracted and transcribed, on-screen text is
  sampled and OCR'd at a fixed interval, and a representative thumbnail
  frame is selected.
\item \textbf{Claim extraction.} An LLM decomposes the transcript, OCR
  text, and caption into atomic claims, each classified as
  \texttt{factual}, \texttt{opinion}, \texttt{prediction},
  \texttt{satire}, or \texttt{rhetorical}, with only \texttt{factual}
  claims specific enough to research marked \texttt{verifiable}.
\item \textbf{Research planning.} For each verifiable claim, an LLM
  proposes 2--5 targeted search queries, explicitly instructed to include
  at least one query aimed at primary sources (official/government
  domains) when plausible.
\item \textbf{Search and fetch.} Each query is run against a search
  provider; result pages are fetched and their full text extracted
  (not just the search snippet).
\item \textbf{Evidence analysis.} For each fetched source, an LLM judges
  stance (\texttt{supports}/\texttt{contradicts}/\texttt{provides\_
  context}/\texttt{irrelevant}) against the claim, grounded in that
  source's actual extracted text only.
\item \textbf{Verdict proposal.} Given the claim and its full evidence
  matrix (every evidence row, with source reliability and stance), an
  LLM proposes a verdict label, confidence, and cited evidence IDs.
\item \textbf{Deterministic validation} (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
\item \textbf{Overall-verdict aggregation.} Once every claim covered by a
  fact-check has a validated verdict, a fixed rule table -- not an LLM
  call -- derives one reel-level verdict.
\item \textbf{Content assembly and rendering.} A carousel of slides
  (headline poster, claims/quote, evidence, conclusion) is assembled,
  drawing almost all content directly from already-validated database
  rows; only headline phrasing and a short ``why'' paragraph pass
  through one additional narrow, schema-constrained LLM call each, both
  with their own grounding checks (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Source tiering}
Every retrieved source is scored along an 8-dimension weighted
reliability rubric and assigned to one of eight tiers:
\texttt{primary\_government}, \texttt{primary\_legal},
\texttt{primary\_data}, \texttt{news\_wire}, \texttt{established\_news},
\texttt{academic}, \texttt{factcheck\_org}, or \texttt{other}. Tier
membership determines both which sources are eligible to be surfaced as
a claim's ``primary source'' citation on the rendered carousel and, in
aggregate, the reliability-weighted confidence of the evidence matrix
handed to the verdict-proposal stage.

\subsection{Multi-claim, reel-level verdicts}
An earlier version of TruthLens built one fact-check per claim; the
current version builds one fact-check per \emph{reel}, covering every
verifiable claim extracted from it, because a single short-form video
routinely makes several claims of differing veracity, and collapsing
them into one per-claim verdict discards exactly the ``partially true,
partially misleading'' structure a reader needs. The rule table in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} governs how per-claim verdicts combine
into the one verdict actually shown on the published carousel.

\section{Methodology: Making the Model More Trustworthy, Not Just More Capable}
\label{sec:methodology}

The paper's title question -- how to make the model ``really better'' for
this purpose -- is, in our experience building TruthLens, substantially a
question about the pipeline around the model rather than the model
itself. This section describes the four mechanisms we found to matter
most.

\subsection{Deterministic anti-hallucination validation}
\label{sec:validation}
After the verdict-proposal LLM call returns a structured
\texttt{VerdictProposal} (label, confidence, reasoning, cited evidence
IDs), TruthLens runs three checks against data already in the database,
none of which invoke an LLM:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Citation existence:} every ID in \texttt{cited\_evidence\_
  ids} must correspond to an evidence row that was actually passed to
  the model in its evidence matrix.
\item \textbf{Source-fetch existence:} every cited evidence row's source
  must have a stored, successfully fetched full-text passage --- a
  citation cannot point at a source the pipeline only planned to fetch.
\item \textbf{Number grounding:} every numeral appearing in the model's
  \texttt{reasoning\_summary} is extracted and checked for appearance in
  at least one cited source's stored passage text.
\end{itemize}

A verdict that fails any check is not discarded -- it is
\emph{downgraded}: persisted with a \texttt{validation\_status} other
than \texttt{passed}, an appended, clearly delimited
\texttt{[VALIDATION NOTE: ...]} explaining what failed, and excluded
from citation lists shown to a reader. This preserves the model's
underlying verdict label (still potentially useful to a human reviewer)
while refusing to let its specific, unverified justification reach a
reader unflagged.

\subsection{The \texttt{RESEARCH\_FAILED} / \texttt{UNVERIFIED} distinction}
\texttt{UNVERIFIED} is a legitimate, informative output: it means the
system genuinely searched and found insufficient evidence to support or
refute a claim. Early in development, TruthLens conflated this with a
second, very different situation -- the search backend itself returning
zero usable results because of a missing API key or provider
misconfiguration -- and both surfaced identically as
\texttt{UNVERIFIED}, indistinguishable to a reader from a considered
null result. We introduced a distinct \texttt{RESEARCH\_FAILED} claim
status, raised only when \emph{all} queries for a claim fail at the
infrastructure level (not when queries succeed but return no relevant
results), and a publication gate that refuses to build a fact-check
whose primary claim is \texttt{RESEARCH\_FAILED}. This is a direct,
domain-specific instance of the broader selective-prediction principle
that a system should distinguish ``I don't know'' from ``I could not
check''.

\subsection{Verification-gated model cascading}
\label{sec:cascade}
Every pipeline stage defaults to a free, locally hosted model. Three
stages -- claim extraction, content generation (headline/why-paragraph),
and verdict proposal -- additionally run a deterministic
\emph{substantiveness check} on the local model's output before
accepting it:

\begin{itemize}
\item Claim extraction: is the extraction \emph{grounded}, i.e.\ do at
  least half of the extracted verifiable claims have a
  \texttt{source\_quote} that is an actual substring of the combined
  transcript/OCR/caption text?
\item Content generation / verdict reasoning: after stripping internal
  citation markup (\texttt{[[evidence\_id=...]]} blocks), does the
  remaining prose contain at least six real words? This check exists
  because we discovered, live, that a locally hosted model would
  sometimes produce a \texttt{reasoning\_summary} composed
  \emph{entirely} of citation markup with no prose at all -- schema-valid
  (a non-empty string), but explaining nothing to a reader.
\end{itemize}

On failure, the same call is retried once against a stronger paid model
(Gemini) with an identical prompt, and the retry (whichever model
produced it) is logged. This differs from a classic cascade
\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} in that the escalation trigger is a
deterministic grounding/substantiveness predicate specific to this
domain's failure modes, not a learned or generic confidence estimate.

\subsection{Deterministic overall-verdict aggregation}
Once every claim covered by a fact-check has a verdict, TruthLens derives
one reel-level verdict via a fixed rule table over the \emph{set} of
per-claim verdict labels (weighted by whether a claim is
``major,'' importance $\geq 0.5$) -- for example, all claims
\texttt{TRUE} yields overall \texttt{TRUE}; a mix containing both
\texttt{TRUE}/\texttt{MOSTLY\_TRUE} and \texttt{FALSE} yields
\texttt{MISLEADING}. This is a plain combinatorial function, not a
further LLM call, so the single highest-stakes number in the product --
the verdict shown on the published carousel's first slide -- remains
fully auditable from already-validated per-claim verdicts rather than
resting on a second, opaque judgment.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{What this section is, and is not}
We report real telemetry recorded in TruthLens's own audit log during
iterative development and live debugging against a small corpus: five
ingested reel records over three distinct underlying videos (by
content hash), yielding 28 extracted claims (16 marked verifiable and
carried through to a verdict at least once), and 21 verdict-generation
events in total -- more than the number of distinct claims because
several claims were re-verdicted after pipeline fixes across iterations.
This is \emph{operational telemetry from a system under active
development}, not a held-out benchmark evaluation: the sample is small,
the units are not independent (the same claim appears multiple times
across pipeline-fix iterations), and every video in the corpus is
Indian political content in a mix of English, Hindi, and Urdu. We report
it because the numbers are real and, we believe, still informative about
where a deterministic-validation, verification-gated-cascade approach
actually spends its safety margin -- but no accuracy or rate reported

\subsection{Deterministic validation catch rate}
Of 21 verdict-generation events, 15 (71.4\%) passed all three
deterministic checks unmodified; 6 (28.6\%) were downgraded -- 5 for an
unsupported statistic (a number in the reasoning not found in any cited
passage) and 1 for a missing/invalid citation. At the distinct-claim
level, 5 of 16 claims (31.3\%) had at least one verdict downgraded during
testing. We read this as evidence that the validation layer described in
Section~\ref{sec:validation} is not a redundant formality: on this
corpus, it materially changed what was published for roughly
three in ten verdict-generation events, every one of which would
otherwise have been schema-valid and passed through unflagged.

\subsection{Verification-gated cascade trigger rate}
The local model's output failed the deterministic substantiveness check,
triggering escalation to Gemini, in 3 of 7 logged claim-extraction
completions (approx.\ 43\%), 2 of 5 content-generation completions (40\%),
and 2 of 8 verdict-proposal completions (25\%). These are small counts
and should not be read as a precise escalation rate for the underlying
local model in general; they are, however, real, and large enough to
show the gate firing routinely rather than rarely -- a free-only,
no-escalation deployment on this corpus would have shipped several
outputs that were schema-valid but substantively empty.

\subsection{Evidence signal-to-noise}
Of 72 evidence rows the evidence-analysis stage judged, 40 (55.6\%) were
classified \texttt{irrelevant} -- the fetched source, despite being
returned by a search query targeted at the specific claim, did not
actually address it. This is consistent with our qualitative finding
(Section~\ref{sec:failuremodes}) that search recall, not claim
reasoning, is the current bottleneck: over half of retrieved-and-read
pages contribute nothing to the eventual verdict.

\subsection{Source tier distribution}
Of sources actually retrieved and scored, the majority (54 of 72 scored
sources) fell into the lowest \texttt{other} tier (mean reliability
0.404), with only 8 reaching \texttt{primary\_government} (mean
reliability 0.674) and none reaching \texttt{primary\_legal} or
\texttt{primary\_data} in this corpus, despite the research-planning
prompt explicitly requesting at least one primary-source-targeted query
per claim. This gap -- the prompt asks for primary sources, but the free
search backend rarely surfaces them, especially for
India-specific government domains -- is, in our assessment, the most
consequential open problem for output quality, ahead of anything in the
LLM reasoning stages themselves; we return to it in
Section~\ref{sec:futurework}.

\subsection{An out-of-sample pilot}
\label{sec:pilot}
Every number above comes from the same handful of videos used during
development. As a first, small step toward the held-out evaluation
Section~\ref{sec:futurework} calls for, we ran TruthLens against two
reels it had never seen, each independently fact-checked by a
professional Indian fact-checking organization (BOOM Live, Alt News),
sourced by fetching the organization's own article and extracting the
original Instagram post it had embedded as evidence -- not by us
selecting or writing the claim or the verdict. On both, TruthLens's
reel-level verdict (\texttt{MOSTLY\_FALSE} in both cases) agreed with
the independent ground truth (\texttt{FALSE} in both cases). We do not
read this as a validated accuracy result -- $n=2$ -- but the process of
running it surfaced something more informative than the label match
itself.

On the second case, the label match was coincidental rather than
earned. The reel's caption never states the claim that was actually
false in words; the misinformation is entirely visual (real World Cup
celebration footage captioned as a protest). TruthLens's text-derived
claim extraction found two tangential claims in the caption's moralizing
language and never identified or checked the one thing that was
actually misleading: what the video shows. \emph{Both} pilot cases follow
this same pattern -- genuine footage, false caption about what it
depicts -- which was also the dominant pattern among the professional
fact-checks we surveyed while sourcing this pilot. TruthLens has no
video-provenance or reverse-image-search capability; its architecture
may be structurally weak against the single most common real-world
failure mode in this domain, independent of anything about LLM
reasoning quality. We treat this as the most important finding of this
pilot and return to it in Section~\ref{sec:futurework}.

The same run also surfaced two further bugs, both fixed the same day
(Section~\ref{sec:failuremodes}): evidence retrieval confused two
similarly-named but distinct organizations, and a verdict the
deterministic validator had already downgraded still had its full
free-text reasoning reused downstream, carrying an entity-confused,
unsupported claim about a real organization's ideology onto a rendered
slide. Neither is visible in the aggregate statistics above, which is
itself worth noting: a system can look healthy on its summary numbers
while a specific, reproducible, reputationally serious failure sits one
level down.

\section{A Taxonomy of Failure Modes Found in Live Testing}
\label{sec:failuremodes}

Beyond the aggregate statistics above, iterative live testing against a
real deployed reel surfaced several specific, reproducible failure modes
that we believe are under-documented in the automated fact-checking
literature at this level of implementation detail, because they only
appear when a system is actually run end-to-end against real content
rather than evaluated on a static benchmark.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Schema-valid, substantively empty output.} Discovered
  independently in three different pipeline stages (claim extraction,
  content generation, verdict reasoning): a locally hosted model
  produces output that satisfies its Pydantic/JSON schema -- a
  non-empty string, a well-typed list -- while containing no actual
  information, e.g.\ a \texttt{reasoning\_summary} composed entirely of
  \texttt{[[evidence\_id=...]]} citation markup with zero prose. No
  schema validation catches this class of failure; only a
  content-level substantiveness check does (Section~\ref{sec:cascade}).
\item \textbf{Verbatim-but-misattributed quotation.} A claim's
  \texttt{source\_quote} can be a genuine, verbatim substring of the
  video's on-screen text -- passing a naive ``is this a real quote''
  check -- while still being the wrong kind of quote: a news-style
  caption written by the video's publisher \emph{about} a person
  (``X Attacks Y Over Z Rule''), not words that person said. Attributing
  such a quote to the person it names, because their name appears
  nearby, produces a fabricated first-person quotation from genuine,
  verbatim text. We fixed this by requiring the candidate speaker's own
  name not to appear inside the quote text itself before trusting the
  attribution -- a person's own quote essentially never refers to them
  self by full name.
\item \textbf{Silent verdict/presentation inconsistency.} After
  introducing reel-level, multi-claim verdict aggregation
  (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), the review dashboard continued
  displaying the primary claim's own individual verdict as ``the''
  verdict, while the actually-published carousel displayed the
  separately computed reel-level verdict. The two could and did
  disagree (one test case: dashboard showed the primary claim's
  \texttt{UNVERIFIED}; the carousel showed the reel's aggregate
  \texttt{MOSTLY\_TRUE}) with no error, because both values were valid
  data -- the bug was in which one the interface trusted, not in either
  computation. This class of failure -- two correct numbers, wrong
  place shown -- will not be caught by any per-field validation test and
  was only found by comparing rendered output against the database
  directly.
\item \textbf{Bracket-matching truncation bug.} A display-sanitization
  regex intended to strip an internal \texttt{[VALIDATION NOTE: ...]}
  suffix used non-greedy bracket matching, which stopped at the first
  \texttt{]} character encountered -- the closing bracket of a Python
  list repr \emph{inside} the note itself (e.g.\ ``Numbers
  \texttt{['3065258', '69']} in reasoning\_summary...''), leaving the
  note's own tail visible on a published slide. Fixed by truncating at
  a literal marker string rather than attempting to bracket-match
  free-form text.
\item \textbf{Unrepresentative thumbnail selection.} A single frame
  extracted at a hardcoded 0.5-second offset is not a reliable
  representative thumbnail; in one case it landed on an unflattering
  extreme close-up mid-gesture. We now sample several candidate frames
  across the video's duration and select by a cheap grayscale-variance
  ``visual complexity'' score, screening out blank/flat/transition
  frames -- not real scene- or face-aware selection, but a
  materially better floor than one fixed guess.
\item \textbf{Entity confusion between similarly-named organizations.}
  Found during the out-of-sample pilot (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}):
  researching a claim about ``Karni Sena,'' evidence retrieval returned
  a Wikipedia article about \emph{Sri Ram Sena} -- a real but different
  Indian organization sharing the common suffix ``Sena.'' The
  evidence-analysis stage labeled this source's stance as
  \texttt{contradicts} rather than \texttt{irrelevant}: an article about
  a different entity does not contradict a claim about this one, it
  simply does not address it. Name-collision entity disambiguation is
  not currently checked anywhere in the pipeline.
\item \textbf{Downgraded reasoning reused as trusted input downstream.}
  Also found during the out-of-sample pilot, and the more serious of the
  two: the verdict-proposal model, given the mismatched Sri Ram Sena
  evidence above, wrote that this ``suggests an unrelated organization
  with violent ideology'' -- a real hallucination the deterministic
  validator correctly downgraded (for an unrelated reason: an unsupported
  number elsewhere in the same reasoning text). But downgrading a
  verdict only forces its \emph{label} to \texttt{UNVERIFIED}; it does
  not redact the free-text \texttt{reasoning\_summary} the model wrote
  to justify its original, rejected verdict, because there is no
  reliable way to isolate just the offending sentence from otherwise
  -reasonable-looking prose. That full text was still being reused
  verbatim in two places -- an evidence card's displayed answer text, and
  as raw input material for the separate overall ``why'' paragraph LLM
  call, which wove it into the actual rendered slide. This is a real
  instance of exactly the failure class the validation architecture
  (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) exists to prevent, in a genuine gap in
  that architecture's coverage: validation applied to whether a verdict
  is safe to \emph{label}, not to whether its reasoning text is safe to
  \emph{quote}. Fixed by treating any non-\texttt{passed}
  \texttt{validation\_status} as disqualifying for reuse of that
  verdict's free text anywhere, replacing it with a generic,
  non-fabricated note instead. This never reached a real audience --
  the fact-check stayed in the internal review queue -- but the
  underlying entity-confusion in evidence retrieval is unfixed and
  could resurface in a form these particular checks don't catch.
\end{itemize}

We consider this taxonomy, and the general lesson that most of these
failures were only discoverable by running the full system against real
content and inspecting rendered output rather than by unit-testing
individual functions in isolation, to be as practically important a
contribution of this work as any single architectural component.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Small, non-independent sample.} The telemetry in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} is drawn from repeated testing against a
handful of real videos, not independent draws from a target population;
several statistics (e.g.\ the 28.6\% validation-downgrade rate) could
shift substantially with a larger, held-out corpus. We report exact
counts, not just percentages, throughout so a reader can judge this
directly.

\textbf{Single domain, single language mix.} All evaluated content is
Indian political short-form video with English/Hindi/Urdu mixed
transcripts. Source-tiering domain lists and the research-planning
prompt's primary-source guidance were not tuned for, and have not been
tested against, other national contexts.

\textbf{No systematic bias audit.} Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork}
discusses documented partisan asymmetry in frontier LLMs; TruthLens
mitigates this only via prompt-level instruction (the neutrality
clause), which is not verified against a labeled bias-evaluation set.

\textbf{No comparison baseline.} We have not run a naive single-shot
``ask an LLM to fact-check this claim'' baseline under identical
conditions to quantify how much the pipeline architecture (versus the
underlying model) contributes to output quality. This is the most
direct way to substantiate the paper's central claim and is the top
item in Section~\ref{sec:futurework}.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A held-out, labeled benchmark.} Curate a labeled set of
  short-form political video claims, disjoint from any content used
  during development, with ground-truth verdicts from independent human
  reviewers (ideally with inter-annotator agreement reported), and
  report precision/recall/F1 against it -- the single change that would
  most improve this paper's evidentiary standing.
\item \textbf{A naive-baseline comparison.} Run a single-shot,
  no-pipeline LLM fact-check over the same claims and report the
  validation-downgrade rate and accuracy difference, to isolate the
  contribution of pipeline architecture from the contribution of the
  underlying model.
\item \textbf{Video-provenance verification.} The out-of-sample pilot
  (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) found both tested cases, and the majority of
  professional fact-checks surveyed while sourcing it, follow the same
  pattern: genuine footage recaptioned with a false claim about what it
  shows or where it's from. TruthLens has no mechanism to check this --
  claim extraction works from transcript/OCR/caption text, and a
  purely-visual false claim (wrong location, wrong event, wrong date) is
  invisible to it regardless of how good the downstream reasoning is.
  Reverse-image/video search against known reference footage, or at
  minimum flagging when a claim about a video's provenance cannot be
  checked from text alone (rather than silently checking a different,
  tangential claim instead), would target what the pilot suggests is the
  dominant real-world failure mode in this domain -- likely higher
  leverage than any of the LLM-reasoning-focused items below.
\item \textbf{Primary-source retrieval.} Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}'s
  most actionable finding: only 8 of 72 retrieved sources in our corpus
  reached the \texttt{primary\_government} tier and none reached
  \texttt{primary\_legal}/\texttt{primary\_data}, despite explicit
  prompt-level requests for primary-source queries. Concrete next
  steps: structured queries against known government/legislative
  document APIs and domain allow-lists (e.g.\ \texttt{.gov.in},
  \texttt{pib.gov.in}, \texttt{indiankanoon.org}) rather than relying on
  a general-purpose web search engine to surface them incidentally.
\item \textbf{Systematic bias evaluation.} Apply an existing partisan-
  asymmetry evaluation methodology (Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork}) to
  TruthLens's own verdicts across politically matched claim pairs.
\item \textbf{Cross-lingual and cross-domain generalization.} Test
  source tiering and research-planning prompts against non-Indian
  political content and languages beyond the current English/Hindi/Urdu
  mix.
\item \textbf{Calibration.} Compare TruthLens's reported confidence
  bands against empirical correctness rates once a labeled benchmark
  exists (Item 1), to check whether ``very high confidence'' verdicts
  are in fact more often correct than ``moderate confidence'' ones.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

TruthLens demonstrates that a substantial fraction of the ``does the
model hallucinate'' problem in LLM-based fact-checking is addressable
architecturally rather than only through better prompting or a more
capable model: a deterministic, non-LLM validation layer applied after
generation caught 28.6\% of generated verdicts in our development
corpus, and an explicit distinction between genuine null results and
infrastructure failure prevents an entire class of false confidence.
These results come from a small, non-independent development corpus and
should be read as preliminary telemetry, not a benchmark evaluation --
but the failure modes they surfaced (schema-valid-but-empty output,
verbatim-but-misattributed quotation, silent presentation
inconsistency) are, we believe, general enough to matter to any team
building an LLM pipeline whose output will be published and trusted.
The most direct path to strengthening every claim in this paper is the
held-out benchmark and naive-baseline comparison described in
Section~\ref{sec:futurework}, which we intend to pursue as this work
continues.
